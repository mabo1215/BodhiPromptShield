Response to the Prior Review (ACM TOPS, submission TOPS-2026-06-0395)
====================================================================

This manuscript was previously reviewed by ACM Transactions on Privacy and
Security and rejected on the Associate Editor's recommendation. Per the PoPETs
submission policy for papers previously reviewed elsewhere, the Associate
Editor's comments are attached and answered point by point below.

The AE's closing sentence stated the requirement precisely: "a clear technical
contribution and a new evaluation on real agent stacks with strong baselines
and realistic privacy attacks." We treated that as the specification for this
revision. The evaluation has been rebuilt from scratch on real agent data with
reproduced comparator systems, and the contribution has been narrowed to the
two mechanisms the evaluation actually isolates. The claims the AE
questioned were re-measured rather than defended. Two did not survive and are
reported as negative results. A third reversed direction: the instrument behind
it turned out not to measure what it was read as measuring, and blind human
annotation gave the opposite answer.


AE comment 1 - Placeholder substitution, local mapping and later restoration
already appear in prior work, including PrivacyRestore and Casper. The authors
do not cite these close systems and do not compare to them. The propagation
framing alone does not constitute sufficient novelty.
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Both systems are now cited and positioned (Section 3.3, Table 2).

Casper's mechanism - rule and NER detection, dummy-value substitution, and
local restoration through a client-held mapping - has been reimplemented and
run head-to-head against our layer under a single protocol, on 300 AI4Privacy
documents through a reference agent, on 493 PrivacyLens trajectories, and in
the downstream task-native evaluation. It appears as "Casper-style" in every
results table, with bootstrap confidence intervals. Installed, version-pinned
Microsoft Presidio, an 8B LLM sanitizer, a spaCy NER baseline, and a GLiNER
transformer PII detector are on the same footing. The outcome is source-
dependent and we report it that way: on AI4Privacy our learned detector reaches
PER 7.4% [6.3, 8.7] against 46.7% for Casper-style and 52.2% for Presidio; on
PrivacyLens, Casper-style (1.4%) and our learned detector (1.8%) are
statistically indistinguishable.

PrivacyRestore is discussed but not reproduced, and we now state the
architectural reason rather than leaving it implicit: it restores content by
steering hidden activations inside the serving model, which presupposes
white-box control of inference. The layer studied here sits in front of a model
it treats as a black box behind an API - which is the deployment position that
makes agent-boundary propagation a problem at all. The two designs occupy
different trust boundaries and cannot be placed on one protocol without
changing what either is.

We accept the AE's judgment that the propagation framing alone is not a
contribution. The contribution list has been rewritten around what the new
experiments isolate: restoration timing as a measured security variable, the
head-to-head propagation comparison, and the surface-form-to-fact leakage gap.
The claim that placeholder substitution is novel has been removed.


AE comment 2 - CPPB contains 256 prompts from only 32 templates. The authors
use full-dataset results for the main claims. This design creates a high risk
of overfitting. Identical development and test scores add to this concern.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Accepted in full. CPPB no longer carries any main-text claim. All CPPB material
- the benchmark card, the baseline comparison, category-wise analysis, the
utility rubric, policy sensitivity, the OCR slice, and latency - has been moved
to Appendix C and is presented for what it is: a controlled surface with known
ground truth on which the routing policy and sanitization modes can be varied.
It is design analysis, not comparative evidence.

Every main-text claim now rests on public external data: AI4Privacy documents,
PrivacyLens trajectories and tasks, and AgentDojo's banking suite, each with
its own splits and its own scoring.


AE comment 3 - The main propagation experiment uses a deterministic reference
agent. On real PrivacyLens trajectories, 83% of sensitive facts remain
semantically exposed. SPE measures literal span survival and misses contextual
disclosure. The authors' own inference attack still reaches 50% accuracy after
sanitization.
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This comment identified what has become the paper's third headline finding,
and the answer changed direction during the revision.

We first did what the comment asks: measure the quantity for all ten methods on
all 493 trajectories rather than in one configuration. On the content-word
overlap criterion - now named Fact Retention Rate and described as a lexical
measure of surviving wording rather than as a measure of inferability -
83.0-98.8% of facts survive every method that leaves usable text behind. We
then built the direct measure the comment implies: a judge model, from a
different family than the downstream agent, shown only the released surface and
asked whether a reader would still learn the fact (3,564 judgments).

The judge agreed with the lexical criterion, and both were wrong. On a
stratified 102-item subset, 17 per method, a person answered that same question
blind - without the judge's verdict, and without knowing which method produced
any item. The judge reaches Cohen's kappa = 0.087, chance, at 58.8% raw
agreement, calling a fact inferable in 81.4% of items where the annotator does
so in 57.8%; the lexical criterion reaches 0.250. Neither can carry a claim
about inferability, and the paper no longer uses them for one.

The human labels reverse the finding those proxies suggested. Factual
inferability falls from 100% [100, 100] with no protection to 52.9%
[29.4, 76.5] under Presidio, 41.2% [17.6, 64.7] under Casper-style, 35.3%
[11.8, 58.8] under our learned detector and 23.5% [5.9, 47.1] under the
heuristic, and every difference from the control has a bootstrap interval clear
of zero. Mediation reduces factual inferability substantially - the opposite of
what a surface metric or an unvalidated judge reports (Section 6.2).

We report the methodological result alongside the empirical one, because it
bears on this comment directly: the 83% figure that prompted it came from an
instrument that does not measure what it was read as measuring, and the judge
model we built to replace that instrument failed the same check while agreeing
with it closely enough that neither exposed the other. Only human labels
separated them. Work measuring semantic leakage with a judge model - ours
included, until this check - should report agreement against human annotation
before treating its output as a measurement.

Since the first version of this response the labels have been checked twice
more, and the check the AE would have asked for now exists. The reported labels
come from one reader, an author, who annotated all 102 items blind to method
and to the judge's verdict. A second reader, not an author, reviewed those
labels and changed four, every one from inferable to not and every one on the
three most aggressive methods; because that pass began from the first reader's
answers, the 95.8% agreement between them is primed and is not a reliability
estimate. A third reader, also not an author, then rated all 102 items from a
blank sheet, seeing neither earlier reader's answers nor the judge's verdict.
That pairing is an inter-rater reliability estimate: Cohen's kappa = 0.739 at
87.3% raw agreement, substantial agreement between two people who could not see
each other, and it reproduces the ordering, giving the heuristic the same 23.5%
and no protection the same 100%.

The consequence for the finding is the part that matters. Scored against the
third reader the judge sits at kappa 0.120 and the lexical criterion at 0.240,
within a few points of the 0.087 and 0.250 they score against the reported
labels. The gap between the two automated measures and a person therefore
survives a rater who saw no earlier round and has no stake in the outcome,
which closes the reading that it came from one annotator's idiosyncrasy. We
continue to report the first reader's labels throughout; the third round is an
independence check, not a replacement data source, so no reported number moved.

We also tested the other reading of this result, that the judge was simply too
small. Three further judges spanning 8B to 70B answered the identical question
on the identical released text, with prompt and decoding held fixed (Appendix
I). Capacity is not irrelevant: the 70B reaches kappa 0.491 against the first
reader where the reported 27B judge reaches 0.087. It is still only moderate
where two independent readers reach substantial, and it agrees with the 27B
barely at all (kappa 0.070), so the two are not measuring the same quantity.
The rates show the failure has a direction that a leak rate hides: annotators
call 57-58% of items inferable, the 8B judge 15%, the two mid-size judges
81-86%, the 70B 37%. A larger judge narrows the gap and does not close it, and
nothing in a judge-scored rate reveals which way it is wrong.

On the deterministic reference agent: it is retained only for the AI4Privacy
source, where it provides a controlled instrumented pipeline. The PrivacyLens
results use that benchmark's real trajectories, and the downstream evaluation
runs a 32B open-weight agent over 200 PrivacyLens tasks scored by the
benchmark's own judge model with its verbatim judging prompts. AgentDojo tasks
execute against its real sandboxed tools.

The consequence for our own claims is reported rather than minimized:
downstream judge-scored leak rates move only 38.0% -> 28.5-37.5% with
overlapping intervals, i.e. no method significantly reduces what the agent
actually discloses under that scoring. We flag that those rates come from
PrivacyLens' own judge model, which we have not validated against human labels;
since our own judge failed exactly that check, the same scrutiny applies to
them.


AE comment 4 - Enterprise redaction achieves lower exposure than the proposed
configuration, 8.1% versus 9.3% PER. The proposed method gains only 0.02 in AC
and TSR. A custom utility rubric without independent validation cannot
establish a meaningful improvement.
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Accepted. The AC/TSR rubric has been retired from the main text and is now
defined and used only on the controlled surface in Appendix C, where its role
is to rank mediation modes against one another under identical prompts.

Main-text utility is now task-native and external. PrivacyLens supplies its own
judge model, verbatim judging prompts, and 0-3 helpfulness scale over 200
public tasks; AgentDojo determines utility and attack success from environment
state after real tool execution. Both are reproducible without any rubric of
ours.

The 8.1% vs 9.3% comparison the AE cites was a property of the controlled
single-boundary surface, which gives no credit for cross-boundary survival or
restoration timing. Appendix C now says so directly instead of conceding a
loss. On the real agent measurements, where those boundaries exist, the
ordering reverses by a factor of six to seven on AI4Privacy.


AE comment 5 - The learned detector reaches only 0.53 Span F1 with 0.36
precision on AI4Privacy. The authors also withhold exact model identities and
omit parts of the OCR and runtime records. These gaps prevent an assessment of
deployment value and do not enable reproducibility.
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
The detector roster has been extended rather than defended. A GLiNER PII
transformer detector and an 8B LLM sanitizer now run in the same protocol, and
the paper states plainly that the logistic-regression detector is not a
contribution: it is the cheapest component that makes the propagation-control
layer measurable, and Table 5 shows stronger detectors slotting into the same
protocol with different failure profiles.

On identities: this was the part of the comment we had not in fact answered,
and it is answered now. Every model is named in the paper, in an appendix
roster giving role, tag, parameter count, quantization, and digest prefix:
qwen3:32b (32.8B, Q4_K_M) as the downstream agent, PrivacyLens' own
mistral:7b-instruct-v0.2 (7.2B, Q4_K_M) as its judge, gemma2:27b (27.2B, Q4_0)
as the inferability judge, deliberately from a third family so that agreement
cannot come from shared weights, qwen3:8b (8.2B, Q4_K_M) as the LLM sanitizer
and abstraction model, urchade/gliner_multi_pii-v1 as the transformer detector,
and en_core_web_lg behind spaCy NER, Presidio, and the Casper-style baseline.
Nothing was routed to a hosted API.

On records: every run behind the rebuilt Results section writes a runtime
manifest recording model identifier, digest, quantization, decoding parameters,
context length, library versions, seeds, and host/GPU. Bootstrap intervals use
1,000 resamples with a fixed seed. The alias-only cross-model
table and the unprovenance OCR claims that prompted this comment have been
removed from the main text along with the rest of the CPPB material.


AE comment 6 - The span-level LDP analysis covers only an optional replacement
step under restrictive assumptions. It gives no guarantee for the evaluated
system.
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Accepted and acted on before this revision. The span-level LDP proposition has
been removed. Section 2.4 now states in two sentences that the evaluated
pipeline is deterministic and offers no end-to-end formal guarantee, and that a
guarantee would require a different mechanism and a proof covering the complete
agent state and restoration path. No formal claim is made anywhere in the
paper.


What changed overall
--------------------
- Ten methods compared under one protocol on real agent data, with bootstrap
  confidence intervals on every reported quantity.
- Downstream evaluation on 200 PrivacyLens tasks with a 32B agent, scored by
  the benchmark's own judge; AgentDojo banking tasks under prompt injection
  with real tool execution.
- Restoration timing isolated as a mechanism, and reported as three columns
  rather than one ratio. Released-context exposure is 1.6% [1.1, 2.1] deferred
  against 51.0% [48.0, 54.0] early, but that quantity is computed from the text
  the layer releases and moves by construction with the restoration point, so
  we no longer present it as a measured security outcome. The agent-dependent
  columns are less one-sided: deferral costs 0.11 helpfulness points, and exact
  identifiers in the final action run the other way, 11.6% against 4.8%,
  because under deferral that column is measured after the authorized boundary
  has restored the values and so counts intended disclosure.
- One negative result reported as such: mediation does not defend against
  prompt injection, on 74 paired task/injection trials.
- One measurement result that reversed a prior claim: a lexical criterion and
  an LLM judge both report that mediation leaves facts intact, both disagree
  with blind human annotation (Cohen's kappa 0.25 and 0.09), and the human
  labels show factual inferability falling from 100% to 24-53% across the
  roster. Downstream judge-scored leakage is statistically unchanged, but it is
  scored by an unvalidated judge and inherits the same doubt.
- The human labels now carry an inter-rater reliability estimate: a third
  reader, not an author, rated all 102 items from a blank sheet and agrees with
  the reported labels at kappa 0.739. Both automated proxies score against that
  reader roughly what they score against the reported labels, and a judge-
  capacity sweep from 8B to 70B narrows the human-machine gap without closing
  it.
- CPPB demoted to an appendix ablation surface; the AC/TSR rubric, the
  alias-only cross-model table, and the disclaimed tables removed from the
  main text.
