Dear Editors,

We submit "BodhiPromptShield: Pre-Inference Prompt Mediation for Surface-Form Privacy Propagation in LLM Agent Pipelines" for consideration by Proceedings on Privacy Enhancing Technologies, Issue 3 of 2027.

The paper addresses a deployment-time privacy problem in LLM agent pipelines. Raw user content does not stay in one model call: it enters retrieval queries, memory writes, tool arguments, OCR-derived text, and logs, and every downstream copy inherits whatever the first write contained. De-identification middleware is normally designed and evaluated at a single document or ingress boundary, which does not measure this failure mode. We treat prompt privacy as a propagation-control problem across agent boundaries and evaluate it accordingly.

BodhiPromptShield is a policy-aware pre-inference mediation layer that detects sensitive spans, routes them to typed placeholders, semantic abstraction, or secure symbolic mapping, and defers exact-value restoration to authorized execution boundaries. The paper makes no cryptographic or differential-privacy claim; it is a systems and measurement contribution.

The four contributions are:

1. A propagation-control formulation with a threat-to-evaluation mapping, and a protocol that measures exposure at each agent boundary rather than once at a document boundary.
2. Restoration timing established as the design variable the layer actually holds. Holding the detector and mapping fixed and moving only the point at which real values re-enter the pipeline changes released-context exposure from 51.0% [48.0, 54.0] to 1.6% [1.1, 2.1], for 0.11 helpfulness points. We report that quantity as what it is - a property of the text the layer releases, which moves by construction with the restoration point - and report the agent-dependent columns beside it, where the picture is less one-sided.
3. A head-to-head comparison of ten mediation methods under one protocol on real agent data, with bootstrap confidence intervals on every reported quantity.
4. A separation of surface-form leakage from factual leakage, with the measure for the second validated against blind human annotation. Both automated proxies fail that check, and the human labels show mediation cutting factual inferability from 100% to 24-53% - a result neither proxy reports.

The evaluation uses 300 AI4Privacy documents through a reference agent, 493 PrivacyLens agent trajectories, 200 PrivacyLens tasks scored by that benchmark's own judge model and verbatim judging prompts with a 32B open-weight agent, and AgentDojo banking tasks executed against its real sandboxed tools under prompt injection. Comparators include installed version-pinned Microsoft Presidio, a reimplemented Casper-style sanitizer, an LLM sanitizer, a GLiNER transformer PII detector, and spaCy NER. Every reported quantity carries a bootstrap confidence interval.

We draw the reviewers' attention to two results the paper reports as findings rather than as caveats. First, measuring factual disclosure is harder than measuring identifiers, and getting it wrong is easy. A word-overlap criterion and an LLM judge both report that mediation leaves facts intact; both disagree with blind human annotation, at Cohen's kappa 0.25 and 0.09, the latter chance agreement. The human labels reverse the conclusion: factual inferability falls from 100% with no protection to 53% under Presidio and 24% under our most aggressive detector, every difference from the control clear of zero. We consider this the paper's most consequential result, and the methodological half of it as much as the empirical half - our own judge produced a plausible, self-consistent and wrong answer, and agreed with a crude lexical proxy closely enough that neither exposed the other. Work measuring semantic leakage with a judge model should report agreement against human annotation before treating its output as a measurement. Second, mediation does not defend against prompt injection: attack success is statistically unchanged across conditions over 74 paired task/injection trials.

The labels have been checked by two further readers, neither an author. The reported labels come from one reader, an author, who annotated all 102 items blind to method and to the judge's verdict. The second reviewed those labels and changed four, every one in the direction of less inferable, so the 95.8% agreement between them is primed and is not a reliability estimate. The third rated all 102 items from a blank sheet, seeing neither earlier reader's answers nor the judge's verdict, and agrees with the reported labels at Cohen's kappa 0.739 - substantial agreement between two people who could not see each other. Scored against that reader the judge and the lexical criterion sit at 0.120 and 0.240, within a few points of what they score against the reported labels, so the distance between the automated measures and a person is not one annotator's idiosyncrasy. Nor is it judge size: three further judges from 8B to 70B answered the same question on the same text, and the largest reaches 0.491 - five times the reported judge, still short of two people. The reported labels are unchanged; the later rounds are independence checks, not a replacement data source.

Relevance to real-world privacy applications. The setting is one that organizations are deploying now: assistants that read a user's document, search an internal index, keep a running memory, and call tools that write to real systems. The paper gives an operator of such a system a measured account of what a mediation layer prevents - literal identifiers reaching retrieval queries, memory writes, tool arguments, and logs before an authorized boundary needs them - and what it does not. The released artifact contains the middleware, the benchmark construction and split scripts, all experiment and figure scripts, the run manifests, and the result records behind every reported number.

Prior review. This manuscript was previously reviewed by ACM Transactions on Privacy and Security (submission TOPS-2026-06-0395) and rejected on the Associate Editor's recommendation. Per the PoPETs policy on papers previously reviewed elsewhere, the Associate Editor's comments are attached together with a point-by-point response. The revision was built directly against that review: the evaluation has been rebuilt on real agent stacks with reproduced comparator systems and realistic attacks, the paper-specific benchmark has been demoted to an appendix ablation surface, the in-house utility rubric has been replaced by task-native external scoring, and the claims the AE questioned were re-measured rather than defended: two did not survive and are reported as negative results, and a third reversed direction once blind human annotation replaced the automated proxy behind it.
